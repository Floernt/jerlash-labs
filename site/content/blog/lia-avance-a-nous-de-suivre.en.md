While some tech leaders are going back to coding so as not to miss the revolution under way, many legal professionals are still debating the “relevance” of AI. By waiting for everything to be regulated, we mainly risk missing the moment. For the question is no longer whether AI will transform the way we work — but how each of us can start engaging with it, right now, at our own scale.

---

When Sergey Brin, co-founder of Google, comes back to code in person at the offices of the company he helped create, it is not to indulge the nostalgia of a reformed geek. It is because, in his words, “the pace of AI exceeds anything we have known in our careers”. While some are still setting up working groups to “reflect on the potential impact of AI”, others are already integrating it into their decisions, their research, their day-to-day trade-offs. This gap can be found in every sector. Law is no exception. It is even emblematic.

In discussions about AI, you often hear the same refrains: “We can’t, for confidentiality reasons”, “it’s not reliable enough yet”, “we’ll see when it’s better regulated”. These objections deserve consideration — not to brush them aside, but to put them to the test. For while some are well founded, many in fact stem from a lack of familiarity with the ground. It is not the technology that is lagging behind, it is the ideas we have of it. It is easier to say that AI “is not suited to law” than to admit that we have not yet taken the time to test a single use case, however modest.

What most often holds people back is not the absence of solutions, it is the idea that one would not know how to use them “properly”. That one is not technical enough, not rigorous enough, not up to date enough to “allow oneself” to use them. This form of self-censorship is all the more pernicious as it is often expressed under the guise of caution or ethics. We prefer to suspend judgement, wait for official approvals, reassure ourselves among peers by repeating that we are “keeping an eye on it”. In reality, we are depriving ourselves of a decisive learning curve. And we leave the field to those who are moving forward — not because they are better, but because they have started.

Starting does not necessarily mean deploying a chatbot or rethinking one’s entire business model. Sometimes it is as simple as spotting a tedious task you repeat every week, and asking whether it could be made easier. Drafting a LinkedIn post with a well-calibrated prompt, automating a thank-you email after a meeting, summarising an overly dense report with a private conversational agent… These gestures seem trivial, but they shift one’s perspective. They create a knock-on effect. They make AI concrete. And above all, they bring a decisive skill back into lawyers’ work: discernment. Not about what the machine can do “instead of” us, but about what is worth doing “with it”.

We can wait for everything to be mapped out, for the fee scales to come down, for the charters to be approved, for the tools to be certified “compatible with regulated professions”. But we can also, quietly, start testing. Asking concrete questions of concrete tools. Documenting what works, what gets stuck, what would deserve a clearer framework. This is not betraying professional ethics, it is preparing their update. And it is, above all, a way of taking ownership of the movement again, instead of being subjected to it.

For the real fault lines are no longer between those who believe in AI and those who do not. They are between those who are already training themselves to work differently, and those who persist in thinking that their current skills will be enough for tomorrow. Law remains a profession of interpretation, strategy and trust — but the conditions of practice are changing. Faster than we think. It is not a question of fashion, nor even of innovation. It is a reshaping of the playing field. And it has already begun.
