Faced with the promises of AI, many lawyers give in to the temptation to “feed it everything”. Case law, legal commentary, notes, emails, templates… everything is poured into the tool, in the hope of a legal super-intelligence. But more does not mean better. And it is often excess that makes AI imprecise or confused. Conversely, a methodical and contextualised approach, brings far more tangible gains with AI as a compass, not as a catch-all.

*** 

An abundance of data is not always synonymous with relevance. In their quest for efficiency, many law firms think that by pouring their entire document base into an AI tool – often a RAG – they will have an infallible machine at their fingertips. But this “put everything in to get everything out” approach quickly runs up against its limits: poorly qualified, redundant or decontextualised data produce noise rather than clarity. Artificial intelligence does not then resolve this confusion, it amplifies it. Yet that is no reason to do everything by hand: used well, AI can actually help to prioritise, to sort, to understand which data are truly useful, and in which situations. Less, but better — with AI as a compass, rather than as a catch-all.

Is this, then, where AI shows its true face? Less spectacular but far more useful: that of an assistant for sorting, structuring and prioritising. A configured tool can analyse a document corpus, detect duplicates, identify gaps, suggest groupings by theme or level of complexity. It can also help to map out use cases: which questions do clients actually ask? Which clauses recur in contracts? Which sources are the most cited or the most reliable? In short, rather than being a confused echo chamber, AI becomes a compass for mapping what exists, spotting weak signals and guiding documentary choices. This preparatory work, often neglected, is nonetheless what then allows AI to be truly relevant in everyday use.

Let us take a simple example: a firm specialising in employment law wants to set up an internal assistant to answer clients’ recurring questions on negotiated terminations. Rather than immediately feeding it all the case law and articles in stock, the team starts by having its base of emails, notes and existing templates analysed. The AI isolates the 15 most frequent questions, detects the most cited sources, and proposes a tight but solid documentary core. The result: an assistant that is faster, more reliable, easier to correct or enrich.

Another scenario: a business law firm wants to structure its shareholders’ agreement templates. AI could be used to generate clauses. But also to map the variants used over the last five years. It highlights problematic wordings, the most frequently used clauses, and helps build a modular library — a solid foundation on which lawyers can genuinely rely.

It is not the technological "magic wand" that transforms a law firm, it is what we do with that technology. 

It is the uses, the trade-offs, the tensions, the creative inspiration it gives rise to. Too often, AI tools are seen as prostheses meant to make output more reliable, to avoid errors or to guarantee compliance. They then become guardians of an existing practice rather than levers for change. It is an understandable reflex but one that quickly reaches its limits. For by asserting itself solely within a defensive logic, AI risks freezing professional practices, standardising reasoning, and reproducing – faster – what was already open to improvement. Instead of being an engine of exploration, it becomes a curator of procedures. 

Rethinking the use of technologies therefore starts by asking not “what can the tool do?”, but “what do we want to do differently?”. This reversal of the question is essential so that artificial intelligence is not simply integrated, but truly made our own: in the service of a project, and not in place of a project.

The trap of AI is not the tool itself, it is the temptation to use it as a mirror of our habits rather than as a lever for transformation. Feeding in everything, unfiltered, is reproducing the reflex of accumulation. But choosing, structuring, contextualising is already thinking differently about one’s relationship to information. And AI can support that, if we call on it at the right moment, for the right reasons. So the point is not to do less for the sake of doing less, but to do better in order to be useful. That is where a real AI strategy for lawyers begins.
