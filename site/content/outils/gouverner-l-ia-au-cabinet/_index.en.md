As a rule, AI use charters tend to repeat the same instructions: “use secure tools”, “have it reviewed”, “do not copy and paste”. These instructions are sound, but they cannot always be checked. They say neither on what conditions a tool was authorised, nor what the person reviewing must be able to do, nor how to show that a check actually took place.

This toolkit starts from that observation. An organisation begins to master AI when it can explain why a tool was authorised, on which data, on what conditions, and how its outputs are checked in practice. Having a charter or a use policy is no longer enough. The toolkit therefore offers a model charter, procedures, decision templates and an oversight protocol, which each organisation adapts to its size and its uses.

## Contents of this version

This is a working version. Once adopted and validated by the organisation, the templates must still be put into practice. The whole is neither an adopted charter, nor a compliance label, nor individual legal advice.

The thresholds, weightings, frequencies and roles found in the various modules are working proposals. Each organisation sets them itself, through its own governance. They are neither legal obligations nor scientifically validated criteria.

## The problem

The principles of existing charters are relevant, but worded in such a way that compliance with them cannot be tested. “Have it reviewed” does not say what the review must cover. “Secure tool” does not say who decided so, on what basis, for what use. The ban on copying and pasting offers no guarantee of quality: flawed reasoning remains flawed once reworded.

The problem is therefore as much a matter of organisation and knowledge as a technical or legal one.

## The working hypothesis

The toolkit rests on a design hypothesis.

A policy built on five elements would improve the conditions in which professional judgement is exercised, compared with a merely declaratory charter:

1. a “situated” approval, that is, one granted to a tool for a specific use, with specific data, a specific configuration and specific users;
2. a classification of use cases according to their possible effects;
3. the identification, in each output, of the decisive statements, those on which the conclusion depends;
4. a real power to challenge for the person exercising oversight;
5. traceability proportionate to what is at stake.

Human oversight is at its centre. It does not consist in waiting for a professional to click “Approve”. It requires competence, access to the relevant material and the power to challenge, amend or refuse.

The aim of this toolkit is to bring together the conditions in which professional judgement can be exercised in a transformed technical environment. The toolkit does not replace that judgement.

## Who it is for

The first audience is Belgian law firms of all sizes. The templates are designed to be easily adapted. Next come in-house legal departments, professional associations and training bodies, in the context of continental European law. The current version is designed first for Belgium; adaptation remains necessary for each organisation and each legal order.

## The recommended path

Governance is put in place first. The charter comes at the end: it is the outcome of a process of reflection, and it anchors the decisions and governance choices made along the way. Adopted at the outset, it would remain a declaration of principles that nothing allows to be checked, as the page [Why charters fail](pourquoi-les-chartes-echouent/) describes.

1. Read this page and [Why charters fail](pourquoi-les-chartes-echouent/).
2. Put [governance](gouvernance/) in place: designate the bodies, set their powers and the delegation of decisions.
3. Classify data with [the data module](donnees/).
4. Set the eliminatory conditions, thresholds and test sets for [approval](homologation/), then decide on each approval for a precise combination: tool, use case, data classes, configuration and the people who use it.
5. Assign the checks of [oversight](supervision/).
6. Organise traceability, incident handling, training and authorisation to use with [operations](operations/).
7. Draft and adopt [the charter](charte/), which records the choices made in steps 2 to 6.
8. Record incidents, changes, assessments and reviews, carry out documented re-examinations, and revise the charter when those choices change.

## The minimum for a small firm

A small firm does not need to put everything in place at once. The proposed operational minimum comes down to six elements:

1. an identified point of contact, and a decision-maker distinct from them for sensitive uses;
2. a matrix of data classes;
3. for each tool and each configuration, a use case sheet and an approval decision;
4. a protocol for checking the content produced and the actions carried out;
5. an incident reporting channel and a secure register;
6. short training, focused on verifying sources and on refusing an output.

One rule applies at every stage: examples and test sets never contain real client data or material covered by professional secrecy. They are based on synthetic scenarios.
