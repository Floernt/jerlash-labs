_Time savings, reliability, compliance. Artificial intelligence promises a great deal — and keeps its promises, in part. But by using it to make practices safer, we forget that it can also reinvent them. Provided we change our stance: less steering, more exploration.

---

For several years now, the promises of artificial intelligence in the legal professions have been piling up. Time savings, greater reliability, automation of repetitive tasks. And yet, behind the productivity figures, a paradox persists: the most technologically equipped firms are not always the most innovative. This gap has a name, borrowed from the economist Robert Solow: it reminds us that computerisation does not automatically translate into a qualitative leap in the organisation of work. In law firms, AI might even sometimes tend to freeze practices rather than reinvent them.

When it arrived in law firms, artificial intelligence was welcomed as an obvious ally. A tool capable of sifting through massive volumes of case law, suggesting arguments, generating standard documents or detecting contractual anomalies. 

On paper, it ticks every box of a dream legal assistant: fast, available, reliable, and able to process information on a scale beyond human reach. Internally, these promises translated into a clear objective: produce better, faster, with less risk. But this framework of use has sometimes masked a broader potential: that of a tool which could also help rethink ways of working, and not only perfect them.

For by focusing on improving what already exists, we risk missing the essential: the capacity of these tools to open up other paths. 

When artificial intelligence is used only to secure, check and standardise, it becomes an extension of quality control. It steers but discovers nothing. By constantly seeking to make deliverables more reliable, we end up freezing processes. The tool then takes precedence over intention: it is no longer the lawyer who shapes the use but the software that dictates the contours of the practice. This tension is familiar in other sectors, notably with ERPs, where one observes that the company will often bend to the software, and not the other way round.

Yet artificial intelligence can be much more than a steering and control tool. It can become a lever for exploration, a professional playground in which to test new ideas, reformulate approaches, imagine other ways of working. Provided we move away from the logic of the finished product, and dare to use AI as a malleable material, adaptable, to be shaped according to our own objectives. Some tools open this path: they allow lawyers to create their own agents, to configure lines of reasoning, to formalise their methods… without depending on an IT department or opaque configuration. The tool no longer imposes itself, it offers itself.

But of course, this exploration still has to be accessible. If taking ownership of a tool requires heavy training, successive approvals or the involvement of technical teams, it immediately loses its spontaneous dimension. What gives an AI that allows exploration its strength is its capacity to adapt to the user’s pace, to unfold in short iterations, to evolve with ideas rather than constrain them. A simple exchange in writing or by voice should be enough to initiate a line of logic, formalise a piece of reasoning, sketch out a scenario. Lawyers must be able to test, adjust, iterate. It is this flexibility that makes it possible to turn the tool into a genuine extension of practice: a kind of on-board laboratory.

Reintroducing experimentation at the heart of practice means giving oneself the freedom to explore one’s own blind spots, to put things differently, to dare more agile methods. 

This is not a regression, it is a reconquest: that of a know-how being redefined through contact with new tools. 

Lawyers thus regain the power to shape the way they work, without giving up confidentiality requirements or the rigour of their practice. Innovation is not born of tools themselves, but of what we dare to do with them.
