Amid the clamour of technological promises, it is tempting to believe that AI could upend even the core of our professions. But that would be to forget one essential thing: what makes a lawyer valuable cannot be coded. This post is for those who want to integrate AI without losing the meaning, delegate without dispossessing themselves, move forward without giving up what makes them irreplaceable.

--- 


**What AI will not change – and just as well**

For some years now, artificial intelligence has been everywhere. We test it, we explore it, we talk about it at every round table. We get excited, sometimes we worry. In law firms, the question is no longer whether AI will transform our practices, but how far, and above all how.

Faced with this technological tidal wave, one stance is called for: neither fantasy nor rejection. AI is neither an existential threat nor a magic wand. It is a tool – powerful, promising, but still poorly understood or used. And above all, it is a tool that only makes sense if it fits within a clear vision of the lawyer’s role.

What we would like to set out here is a conviction forged by experience: AI can help us do things, but it will never replace what lies at the heart of our profession. It can structure, synthesise, automate. But it can neither think in our place, nor bear the risk, nor feel the human side.

This post is for those who are asking themselves questions. Those who want to understand what AI changes – and what it does not change. 

Those who refuse to delegate their thinking to the fashion of the moment. 

Our intention is simple: to offer a few reference points, without dogmatism. For more than ever, we need to know where we are irreplaceable. And why.

**What AI does well – and what it saves us**

Let us start by acknowledging the real contributions. Generative AI, in its various forms, already allows us to save time on certain tasks. It reads faster than we do, rephrases without tiring, spots inconsistencies in long texts, offers us outlines or variants when we are going round in circles. 

It can condense complex litigation, generate a first contractual draft, or structure a legal argument according to our instructions.

These are undeniable efficiency gains. And we would be wrong to neglect them. For in a daily routine that is often saturated, where room for manoeuvre is shrinking, this kind of delegation – properly supervised – frees us to concentrate better on what is essential.

It must be said plainly: when it is used well, AI can ease the mental load, smooth out tedious tasks, spare us from rewriting a standard email or a standard contract for the tenth time. It is not there to diminish us. It is there to strengthen us, provided we know how to ask it the right questions and never entrust it with more than it can handle.

But for that, we need a clear understanding of it. Knowing where it excels – in repetition, structured analysis, the generation of standard content – and where it stops.

**What AI cannot do – because it is our profession**

As the tools become more refined, a misunderstanding is setting in: the belief that AI could, in time, reason like us. It must be said clearly: AI does not reason. It calculates, it extrapolates, it combines. It is capable of generating very convincing answers, sometimes even impressive ones. But it does not understand what it is saying.

It has no point of view, no embodied memory, no responsibility. It does not know what a risk is – it merely models it from past data. It feels neither the pressure of time, nor the doubt of an agonising choice, nor the impact a decision will have on a person, a team, a company. 

All of that is our territory. And that is where the difference is made.

Our profession is not only “knowing the law”. It is knowing what to do with it.

It is choosing a path through a maze of legal, political and relational options. 

It is guessing what the client is not yet saying, interpreting a dynamic, sensing when to speak and when to stay silent. It is weighing a strategy not only according to the text, but according to the context. It is understanding that sometimes, _not asserting a right is wiser than exercising it_.

No AI can:

- Hear a hesitation in a voice and infer a need for reassurance;  
- Decide that a conciliatory tone will be more effective than a firm one, at that moment, with that client;  
- Take responsibility for recommending a risky but opportune course, in a case file that involves a long-term vision;  
- Distinguish between what is legal, what is prudent, and what is just;  
- Resist an automatic response because a detail in the case file changes the whole analysis;

In other words, AI can assist us. But it cannot decide. It cannot take ownership. It cannot commit itself. And that, ultimately, is the essence of our function: to carry, to embody, to take responsibility. Not only to “state the law”, but to bring it to life in concrete, human, shifting situations.

And that part, the part of discernment, of positioning, of relationship, we must neither delegate nor water down. It is our real value. And it begins precisely where AI stops. Recognising these limits is not a defensive reflex. It is a condition of mastery. For it is precisely by knowing the contours of what AI cannot – or must not – do that we can decide consciously what we delegate to it. Conversely, if we cross these lines without paying attention, we expose ourselves to another set of risks: those that do not come from the tool itself, but from poorly framed use.

**The risks of poorly framed use**

It is not the tool that is the problem. It is the use we make of it — or that we are pushed to make of it too quickly. Behind the technological enthusiasm, there is sometimes a kind of haste: we test, we marvel, and we forget to set the rules. Yet in a law firm, this carelessness is not trivial. It can be risky, for us as well as for our clients.

The first risk is the illusion of competence. AI can give the impression of “answering well”. But a fluent answer is not a reliable answer. A well-written memo can contain a substantive error. And an analysis without context can miss the essential point. If we let AI draft without checking, we become dependent on knowledge we no longer master.

The second risk is bias AI works from past data, often opaque, sometimes biased. It does not understand law as a dynamic reasoning process, but as a static database. It can perpetuate stereotypes, erroneous interpretations, or propose solutions unsuited to our law. And we will not necessarily see it straight away.

The third risk, more structural, is the dilution of our responsibility. By automating certain tasks, we may be tempted to offload onto the machine. But the machine will never be sued. It will never be bound by confidentiality, nor by professional secrecy. It will not own up to an error, nor will its liability be engaged. It is we who sign, who speak, who bear the weight of a decision.

Finally, there is a subtler risk, that of the impoverishment of meaning. By equipping every gesture with tools, by segmenting every line of reasoning, we may end up dehumanising our profession. Forgetting that it rests first and foremost on a capacity to listen, to adapt, to invent. And that it is precisely there, in this part that cannot be automated, that the client’s trust is forged.

AI is not an enemy. But if we adopt it without a framework, it can make us lose the thread. The thread of our responsibility, of our rigour, of our critical sense. And ultimately, that of our profession.

**Giving the lawyer back their role as pilot**

Faced with these upheavals, we have two options: to be subjected to AI, or to pilot it. Being subjected to it means adopting tools without thinking about their impact. It means delegating key tasks for the sake of speed or comfort. It means letting ourselves be impressed by technological promises without asking whether they match our way of working, our values, our responsibility.

Piloting is something else altogether. It means taking back control. It means recognising AI for what it is: an operational lever, powerful, but one that only makes sense within an organisation that is thought through and
structured. It means choosing what we want to automate, with what level of supervision, in what service logic — and above all, with what objective.

This requires us to position ourselves. To know where we create the most human value, and to concentrate our efforts there. To review our modes of production, our internal processes, our economic trade-offs. To develop our teams, not in order to replace, but to redeploy skills.

Piloting AI also means accepting a new role: that of coordinator. More than ever, we are becoming the conductors of our firm. It is not the machine that sets the tone. It is we who integrate tools into a coherent whole, in the service of a project, a client, a vision.

And this development is fruitful. It forces us to clarify what we want to be. To refocus on discernment, strategy, relationships, advice. To take on a more cross-cutting, more impactful role — less executor, more decision-maker.
