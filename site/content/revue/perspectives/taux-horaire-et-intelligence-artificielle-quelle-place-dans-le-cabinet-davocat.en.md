Generative artificial intelligence is reshuffling the deck: some tasks are being automated, others are disappearing, and time spent is no longer a viable way of valuing one’s services. Does that mean we should bury hourly billing? Not so fast. It is not the hourly rate that is the problem, it is its use by default. In the age of AI, it becomes a choice and not a reflex. Still, you need to know when (and how) it remains relevant.

---

**It is not AI that threatens the hourly rate. It is the use we make of it.**

We often hear that artificial intelligence will “kill” billing by time spent. That clients will no longer agree to pay for an hour what can be generated in a minute. And that firms will, willingly or not, have to switch to fixed fees, subscriptions, or “value pricing”. This tune is not new but it takes on a particular resonance in the age of generative AI.

And yet the hourly rate is not disappearing. It is adapting. Because not everything can be automated. And above all, not everything can be valued by the task. What is changing is that time spent is no longer enough to justify an invoice. You have to be able to say what you really contributed and why that work, even if quick, was worth the price.

## Two narratives, two blind spots

In the debates around AI, two narratives clash. Each contains a share of truth, but neither tells the whole story.

The first is that of the threat. Generative AI makes it possible to produce faster, to summarise, to automate. A memo that took three hours can be generated in ten minutes, a standardised contract drafted from a few instructions. The result: a collapse in “time spent”, and with it, in turnover. Some firms are worried: should they compensate by artificially inflating hourly rates? Behind this worry lies a confusion: believing that the value of work is measured solely by its duration. Yet just because a tool makes it possible to work fast does not cancel out the competence of the person who knows how to do it well. And just because the client pays for less time does not mean they expect less value.

The second narrative, more appealing, sees AI as an opportunity. By relieving lawyers of repetitive tasks, it gives them back time for what matters: analysing, deciding, negotiating, advising. Less mechanical production, more reasoning. Less execution, more discernment. The lawyer would become a “conductor”, a strategist, a final editor. And the billed hour would be worth more, because it would be denser. It is a fine vision but one that presupposes a real shift: cultural, organisational, economic. Because moving from a model based on quantity to one based on quality cannot be decreed. Fee scales have to be revised, the value delivered made explicit, clients educated to a new reading of fees. And above all, one has to accept that certain engagements, once profitable through sheer volume, will no longer be so.

## What the hourly rate still measures and what it no longer captures

Should billing by time spent be abandoned? Not necessarily. But its use needs to be clarified. The hourly rate remains relevant in a certain number of situations provided you know what it measures.

It remains suitable when the service is open-ended, unpredictable, highly changeable: real-time support, a complex negotiation, a crisis. In these cases, time spent remains a good proxy for actual commitment, for the back-and-forth, for the unexpected. The point is not so much to sell a task as to absorb availability, involvement, continuous attention. There, the hourly rate keeps its meaning, provided it is transparent, explained, put in context.

But as soon as the service is recurring, standardisable, or governed by a stable methodology, time is no longer the right metric. Why bill three hours for a task that may, tomorrow, be semi-automated, or even entirely handled by a conversational agent? It is in these areas that the hourly rate becomes fragile not morally but economically. It gives the impression of a gap between the invoice and the reality of the effort.

Between the two, there is a field for experimentation: modular fixed fees, subscription systems, hybrid “units” that combine time spent and value delivered. The point is not to revolutionise your firm overnight but to test, document and adjust. And above all, to be able to explain to clients what they are paying for: not minutes, but competence, responsibility, a result.

## What now? Test, explain, reposition

What AI disrupts is not only the way of working. It is the way of making that work understood. The hourly rate, taken in isolation, becomes too poor a language to explain the value of a legal intervention. It needs to be accompanied by context, clarity, explanation. Saying what an hour contains. What it presupposes upstream. What it prevents downstream.

This also means accepting a period of adjustment. Testing new formats. Moving from “all hourly rate” to a more hybrid scale, where you choose your measuring tools the way you choose a means of transport: according to the journey, not out of habit.

There will always be clients attached to the transparency of time spent. And others who will prefer a logic of results, of deliverables, of subscriptions. What matters is not imposing a model, but being able to discuss it. And not letting the tool, AI or otherwise, set the rules of the game on its own.
