> The arrival of generative AI is not just one more technical advance in the arsenal of tools used by law firms. It marks a genuine paradigm shift: for the first time, we can entrust the known to machines, and devote our energy to what our profession is really about: thinking the unknown, guiding, and committing ourselves.

---

The more time passes, the clearer it becomes that we are living through a turning point. Generative artificial intelligence is not just one more wave in the great cycle of technological innovation. It marks a change of era. In law firms, this transformation cannot be reduced to a few one-off automations or one more tool on the shelf. It forces us to rethink our relationship with time, with production, with value  and above all, with what makes up the role of a lawyer.

For some years now, every technological advance has already promised to “revolutionise” the profession. Digitised archives, collaborative platforms, document management software… At each stage, the talk was of saving time. With generative AI, we cross a threshold: we are no longer simply talking about speeding things up or lightening the load. We are talking about delegating certain repetitive parts of our work in order to  devote ourselves better to what really matters.

Technology is often presented as a linear succession of tools, each a little more powerful than the last. But if you look more closely, each stage of this evolution has transformed a very specific part of the value chain, without ever touching its core: intellectual production.

The first practice management software made it possible to organise case files better, to keep track of billing, to optimise internal logistics. It brought real administrative comfort but had no major impact  on the way advice or advocacy is produced.

Along the same lines, the arrival of digitised legal databases changed legal research. Lawyers could access case law and legal scholarship faster, without having to go “to the library at the Palais”. They could compare sources and look more deeply into certain points. But even this advance, however valuable, did not cross the boundary of final production. Research remained a means never an end and did not “deliver” content directly to the client.

Today, with generative artificial intelligence, we are touching for the first time what had until now been considered inalienable: the very process of drawing up the content intended for the client. The famous “last line” of the production chain.

It is no longer only a question of “how do I find the information?”, but “how do I turn it into advice, into a position, into a document?”.

And this is where the paradigm tips over. Because the lawyer is not there to produce law for the pleasure of technique. The lawyer is there to defend, to protect, to guide. To give direction in situations that are often delicate, adversarial, sometimes painful, or simply strategic.

If we had to sum up in one sentence the real promise of AI for lawyers, it might be this one: **delegate the known in order to think the unknown better**.

A large part of a lawyer’s work has always consisted of handling recurring elements: templates, clauses, case law, tried and tested lines of argument. These “known blocks” are essential but they take a considerable amount of time to be adapted to the situation and consume a precious share of our attention.

Generative artificial intelligence now makes it possible to automate these sequences. 

Let us take a simple example: a client consults you about a shareholders’ agreement. Until now, you would start by taking a template, rereading each clause, adjusting, rewording. With AI, you can now generate a basic version in a few minutes, which you will then refine. You do not disappear from the process; on the contrary, you take back control earlier, at the point where it is a matter of adapting the strategy, anticipating potential conflicts, adjusting to the parties’ real intentions.

Another example: a tax risk analysis of an international structure. AI can sort the information, summarise the case law, prepare a matrix of red flags. The lawyer, for their part, steps in to decide, to judge what is acceptable, to take a position that commits them towards the client.

By freeing up these hours spent on the “known”, we regain time but above all mental space, bandwidth as some would say, to do what nobody else can do in our place: think the unknown. Anticipate, negotiate, persuade or reassure.

AI is neither a gadget nor an existential threat. It is an invitation. An invitation to ask ourselves what, in our practice, truly deserves our time and our intelligence.

We have spent years perfecting our processes, accumulating tools, fine-tuning our methods. But today, the question is no longer to go faster in order to produce more. It is to go further in order to think better.

By agreeing to delegate the known, we create the space needed to explore, to innovate, to guide our clients where no machine will ever be able to go: into the uncertain, the human, the sensitive. That is surely one promise of this new era: to remind us why we chose this profession. And to give us the means to return to it, more available, more clear-sighted, more useful.
