**What if our main difficulty with artificial intelligence were not understanding how it works, but managing to think about what it makes possible?** That, in essence, is the invitation Richard Susskind extends in *How to Think About AI: A Guide for the Perplexed*.

Known for his forward-looking work on the future of the legal professions, Susskind offers here a reflection of broader scope. His ambition is not to provide a technical introduction to artificial intelligence, but to transform the frameworks of thought through which we approach it.

In this respect, the book is a contribution to the debates on the future of law and of the legal professions. It invites us to move beyond two equally reductive attitudes: regarding AI as a mere instrument for improving existing practices, or dismissing it because of its current shortcomings.

But this approach also raises a fundamental question: **is a machine's ability to produce a result comparable to a human's enough to justify entrusting it with an activity that was hitherto human?**

## 1. Technology myopia: the mistake of judging tomorrow by today

One of the fruitful concepts in the book is *technology myopia*.

Susskind uses the term for the tendency to assess the future potential of a technology solely on the basis of its present limitations. The imperfections observed today are then implicitly treated as permanent features.

This bias runs through much of today's debate on generative AI. Hallucinations, reasoning errors and difficulties with certain complex problems are frequently invoked to play down, or even deny, the transformative potential of these systems.

Yet a current technical limitation is not necessarily a lasting impossibility.

Susskind also points out how problematic it is to pass final judgement on technologies that their critics have sometimes never really tried.

This criticism is particularly relevant in the legal world, where discussions of AI often swing between technological enthusiasm and professional scepticism.

However, the argument deserves to be taken further.

If current limitations do not determine the future capabilities of a technology, the reverse is also true: its past progress does not guarantee its future performance. Denouncing technology myopia cannot therefore stand in for a demonstration that the announced transformations are feasible.

Three questions should be distinguished: what the technology allows today, what it might allow tomorrow, and what it would be desirable to have it do.

From this perspective, technological foresight dispenses with neither empirical assessment nor deliberation.

## 2. Understanding processes or assessing outcomes?

One distinction structures Susskind's thinking: that between *process-thinking* and *outcome-thinking*.

The former focus on the inner workings of systems, their mechanisms and their architecture. The latter focus on the results obtained, the functions performed and their consequences.

Susskind illustrates this contrast through the positions of Noam Chomsky and Henry Kissinger on artificial intelligence.

This distinction helps explain why two observers can reach radically different judgements about the same system.

The first may consider that a generative model does not truly understand language, while the second observes that it effectively performs tasks that traditionally presuppose linguistic understanding.

These statements are not necessarily contradictory. They concern two distinct objects: the mechanism and the performance.

This difference becomes particularly interesting when applied to intellectual activities.

Must a system actually understand the law to produce a relevant legal analysis? Must it possess a faculty of judgement similar to a judge's to contribute usefully to resolving a dispute?

Susskind invites us not to dismiss the relevance of a result too quickly merely because the process that produced it differs from the human process.

This proposal is a welcome critique of a certain form of technological anthropocentrism: we spontaneously tend to measure machines against our own faculties.

**But in the legal field, the distinction between process and outcome meets a limit: some processes have a normative value of their own.**

Respect for the adversarial principle, impartiality, the reasoning of a decision or procedural safeguards are not mere means of reaching a legally correct solution. They are part of the very legitimacy of that solution.

A decision does not necessarily become just because its outcome seems satisfactory.

The outcome-oriented approach must therefore be complemented by reflection on the procedural, institutional and human conditions under which outcomes are produced.

## 3. Judgement and quasi-judgement: should we think of machines as humans?

Susskind introduces another interesting distinction, between human judgement and what he calls *quasi-judgement*.

His reasoning consists in distinguishing the process of judgement from one of its functional results: the reduction of uncertainty.

The idea is that a machine could help resolve an uncertain situation without possessing the human faculties traditionally associated with judgement.

This distinction is part of a broader reflection on the difficulties of the language used to describe artificial intelligence. We draw on notions such as intelligence, understanding, empathy or creativity, even though these concepts were historically built to describe human experiences and faculties.

Using intermediate notions, such as quasi-judgement, has the merit of recognising functional capabilities without assuming that human and artificial processes are identical. It thus avoids two pitfalls: wrongly attributing human faculties to machines, or denying them any competence because they do not possess those faculties.

However, in legal activities, judgement cannot be reduced entirely to an operation of reducing uncertainty.

Judging also means interpreting norms, ranking values, taking responsibility and, in some contexts, answering for one's decision to others.

A machine can take part in formulating a solution without occupying the same institutional, moral or legal position as a human being.

We can go on asking whether artificial systems can produce the functional effects of judgement, but we must not forget to think about **how those effects fit with the human and institutional requirements of responsibility**.

## 4. Automation, innovation, elimination: rethinking problems rather than solutions

The distinction between automation, innovation and elimination is probably one of the book's most directly practical contributions.

Automation consists in entrusting a technology with a task already carried out according to an existing process.

Innovation makes it possible to devise new ways of reaching an objective.

Elimination leads, more radically, to the disappearance of the need that an activity met.

This last perspective profoundly changes the way technological transformations are conceived.

In a law firm, automation might consist in speeding up the drafting of a contract. Innovation might lead to designing an interactive tool for building a contractual relationship. Elimination might aim to prevent certain conflict situations that previously required corrective intervention.

These examples illustrate the three possible levels of transformation, without prejudging their technical or legal feasibility.

The issue is no longer only how to carry out an activity faster, but whether the activity itself is necessary.

This reflection is extended by one of Susskind's metaphors: rather than trying to change the wheel of a moving vehicle, why not build a new vehicle running alongside the old one, before gradually transferring the passengers to it?

The image conveys a transformation deeper than the mere improvement of professional tools.

Applied to justice, it invites us not to limit innovation to digitising procedures or automating legal tasks. It leads us to question the very organisation of access to law, of the prevention of disputes and of their resolution.

This perspective nevertheless carries a risk: assuming that radical transformation necessarily represents progress.

The disappearance of a task does not always mean the disappearance of the social need it met. Some professional activities fulfil relational, symbolic or institutional functions that their functional description reveals only imperfectly.

It is therefore important to determine what we really seek to preserve, to transform or to abandon.

## 5. The blind spot: the transformation of the human being in the technical relationship

The main limitation we can identify in Susskind's approach lies not in his analysis of technological capabilities, but in the place given to the transformation of the individuals who use them.

By focusing on outcomes and functions, his analytical framework risks leaving in the background a question at least as important: what becomes of the human being who carries out an intellectual activity by means of a generative system?

This question goes beyond professional substitution.

Regular use of an AI system can change working methods, habits of reasoning, criteria of trust and ways of learning. It can lead to an increase in individual capabilities, but also, depending on the conditions of its integration, to a form of dependence or to the erosion of know-how.

Gilbert Simondon's work on individuation and Bernard Stiegler's work on proletarianisation offer complementary perspectives here.

Technology is not merely an instrument external to the individual. It helps structure their activity, their knowledge and their relationships with the world.

From this angle, an inquiry centred on outcomes seems insufficient.

Two systems can produce comparable outcomes while bringing about radically different transformations in their users. One may support the development of new skills, while the other may foster growing intellectual dependence.

This observation invites us to move beyond the opposition between process and outcome.

**We must also think about what using an AI system makes us become.**

For the legal professions, this question is central: how can technical environments be designed that increase capacities for analysis and decision without gradually disengaging professionals from their own intellectual activity?

## Conclusion — Thinking about the possible without giving up on ends

*How to Think About AI* is of major interest: it forces the reader out of contemplating present limits to examine future transformations.

Susskind usefully reminds us that the most structuring technologies are not necessarily those that most faithfully reproduce our practices, but those that change the way we define and solve problems.

This approach is particularly fruitful for the legal professions, whose organisational models still rest largely on historical structures.

But thinking about the future is not only a matter of identifying what becomes technically possible. We must also determine what remains humanly desirable and legally legitimate.

Susskind's reflection thus calls for an extension: a theory of the integration of AI that is limited neither to the performance of systems nor to their substitution for existing activities, but that examines the reciprocal transformations of individuals, institutions and their technical environments.
