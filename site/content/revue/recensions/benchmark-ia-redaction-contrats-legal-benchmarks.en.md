Legal Benchmarks describes itself as an independent body evaluating legal AI tools. It was founded by Anna Guo, a former large-firm associate and in-house lawyer. Its “Phase 2” study, published in September 2025, is one of the few to place lawyers and AI tools side by side on the same contract drafting tasks, and to measure a third configuration as well: the lawyer assisted by AI. It is this third measurement that brought us back to a text more than a year old.

## What the study says

Thirteen AI tools and lawyers handled thirty contract drafting tasks between July and August 2025. The published summary reports four results.

On the reliability of the outputs, the tools score 57% on average, the lawyers 56.7%. Lawyers assisted by AI rise to 61.5%.

On the usefulness of the outputs, rated on a scale of 3 to 9, lawyers are slightly ahead of the tools: 7.53 against 7.25.

The best tools taken individually reach around 73% reliability, against 70% for the best lawyer. The summary gives two similar figures for the best tools (“73.3% and roughly 73%”) without saying whether they refer to two distinct tools. The clearest gap concerns time: a little under thirteen minutes on average per task for the lawyer (12 min 43 s), less than a minute for the tool. According to the authors, the human advantage lies in drafting that requires context and judgement.

## Discussion

### A tie that mainly says the task is difficult

The figure that will circulate is the tie between AI and the lawyer: 57% against 56.7%. In our view, the most instructive figure lies elsewhere. It is in the absolute level. An average reliability of 57% means that, according to the criterion used, more than four outputs in ten are not reliable, whether they come from a machine or from a fellow lawyer. The best lawyer in the sample tops out at 70%.

Two readings are possible. Either the tasks were very demanding, or the reliability criterion is strict. Without the definition of this criterion, it is impossible to decide, and that is the study's first problem.

### What the summary does not give

The published page contains neither the list of the thirteen tools, nor their version, nor the number of lawyers, their seniority or their jurisdiction. It does not define the “reliability rate”, does not describe the usefulness grid from 3 to 9, does not say who did the scoring, nor whether the scoring was blind. No confidence interval accompanies the gaps. The “full report” link points back to the same page.

We looked for this information in the site's methodology pages. They describe the current method of the rankings (November 2026 cycle), which rests on a “per-task success rate” and automated judges, and not the 2025 method. The site itself warns that the two measurements are not comparable and that the 2025 reports followed “an earlier methodology”, which is described nowhere.

Let us take the gap between 57% and 56.7%. Over thirty tasks, and without knowing the number of lawyers or the spread of the results, nothing allows us to say that this 0.3-point difference exists. The honest finding is that the two averages cannot be told apart. The 4.8-point gap in favour of the assisted lawyer is more interesting, but it suffers from the same limitation, made worse by the absence of any description of the assistance protocol: which tool, at what point, with what instruction.

### The evaluator's business model

Legal Benchmarks states that it has no sponsor among vendors and specifies that vendors can neither preview nor modify the results. The site also indicates that vendors pay evaluation fees, can order private reports, obtain certifications and use consulting services. We are not writing here that the results are biased: nothing on the page suggests so. We do consider, however, that an evaluator paid by the players it evaluates must publish its method in full so that its declaration of independence can be verified. For the 2025 study, this is not the case.

## Relevance for lawyers

For a lawyer wondering whether to entrust first drafts of contracts to a tool, the study gives useful information, provided it is read correctly. It does not show that AI drafts as well as a lawyer. It shows that, on this set of tasks, neither one nor the other alone produces a reliable text in a reassuring proportion, and that the combination of the two does a little better. The time saving is massive; the quality gain is modest and poorly established.

The practical consequence is, in our view, the following: the time saved on drafting must be reinvested in review, otherwise the firm trades twelve minutes for an unchanged error rate.

For the jerlash.labs AI & trust research programme, the study asks the right question, that of the assisted lawyer rather than the human-versus-machine duel, without providing the means to answer it. A replication should at least publish the definition of the reliability criterion, the usefulness grid, the number and profile of participants, the assistance protocol and the spread of the results. We will come back to these conditions in the programme's evaluation protocol. [À VÉRIFIER : engagement à confirmer par l'auteur.]
