The article reviewed is an opinion piece sponsored by LexisNexis.

## 1. Technology adoption faster than its governance

Artificial intelligence has already entered the heart of legal work. Yet the structures responsible for overseeing its use seem to be evolving more slowly than professional practice.

This is the observation made by Karen Waldron in an article published on 29 October 2026 in *Artificial Lawyer*.

The author reports several findings from a LexisNexis survey: 94% of the professionals surveyed reportedly now use AI, whereas only 29% say it is embedded in their organisation's strategy and operations. In addition, 43% say they are progressing slowly, in a still largely experimental way.

These findings, whose methodological characteristics are not detailed in the article, illustrate a tension: **the use of AI is becoming widespread before organisations have fully defined the conditions for keeping it under control.**

This development no longer concerns administrative tasks alone. According to the data reported, legal research accounts for 69% of uses, followed by document summarisation (62%). Drafting and document review are also widely affected.

The challenge is to govern practices that contribute directly to the formation of legal analysis.

## 2. A transformation of the conditions in which judgement is produced

The article opens with an interesting observation: two experienced lawyers can analyse the same case file and reach different recommendations, in particular because their experience and judgement differ.

The introduction of AI adds a new variable: the technical systems used.

A research system influences which authorities are consulted first. A drafting tool proposes a first contractual wording. A summarisation system determines which facts or risks will be made visible.

AI no longer intervenes only in the execution of an already defined task. It can also help determine the information taken into account, the alternatives considered and the representations on the basis of which the lawyer reasons.

There is thus a fundamental distinction between two forms of governance:

- **Technical governance**, which covers security, vendors, access, confidentiality and compliance.
- **Epistemic governance**, which concerns the conditions under which the knowledge used in professional activity is produced, validated and made one's own.

The second cannot be reduced to the first.

A system can meet security and confidentiality requirements while producing problematic effects on its users' judgement.

## 3. Human oversight is not reducible to formal responsibility

Karen Waldron points out that lawyers remain responsible for their advice, even when AI is involved in preparing it. She also stresses the importance of checking generated output, identifying uncertainties and maintaining critical skills.

These proposals are relevant, but the reflection can be taken further.

Professional responsibility does not, by itself, guarantee that effective oversight exists.

A lawyer can remain legally responsible for an analysis without having, in practice, all the conditions needed to check its foundations. It must then be examined whether that person really has the means to understand, challenge and correct it.

This capacity depends in particular on access to sources, available time, experience, the organisation of work and the possibility of rejecting a generated proposal.

**Human oversight should therefore be seen as an effective professional capacity, rather than as a mere validation step.**

This distinction is a central focus of the jerlash.labs *Human Agency & AI* programme.

## 4. The risk of skills dispossession

One of the most interesting passages concerns the training of young lawyers.

According to the survey cited, 75% of respondents fear that intensive use of AI may compromise the development of legal judgement among junior associates.

The author draws a distinction: the problem does not necessarily lie in the disappearance of the repetitive tasks traditionally entrusted to junior associates, but in the possible disappearance of the learning opportunities they provided.

This remark makes it possible to move beyond a simplistic opposition between automation and the preservation of traditional methods. Not all tasks removed by automation deserve to be kept. On the other hand, some of the intellectual operations associated with them can play a decisive role in acquiring expertise.

Legal research, for example, does not consist solely in finding a reference. It also involves understanding the structure of a legal order, discovering contradictions, assessing the relevance of sources, discriminating between them, and gradually building an interpretation.

When AI performs some of these operations, one must determine what the lawyer stops doing, but also what they may stop learning.

It would, however, be excessive to equate all cognitive delegation with dispossession. A technical system can also enrich learning opportunities and strengthen professional capacities.

The article opens this discussion without really proposing a method for assessing these effects.

## 5. The limits of the proposed approach

Karen Waldron's main contribution lies in her call for operational governance, proportionate to the risks and regularly updated.

She stresses the appointment of owners, the establishment of understandable rules and the adaptation of controls to the nature of the tasks.

Three limits nevertheless deserve to be highlighted.

**First, the absence of a sufficiently detailed empirical analysis.** The statistics used are interesting, but the article does not provide the information needed to assess their representativeness, the wording of the questions or the sampling method. They must be understood as reported findings, not as a definitively established description of the profession as a whole.

**Second, the priority given to organisational governance.** Clarifying responsibilities is an important but insufficient condition. An organisation can have a formal policy without its users being genuinely able to detect a plausible error or to question a recommendation.

**Third, the absence of criteria for assessing the effects of AI on professional autonomy.** The article recommends training and supervision, but does not propose a method for determining whether the practices adopted actually strengthen the capacity for judgement.

Finally, the text is a publication sponsored by LexisNexis, which sells legal AI solutions. This commercial dimension must be taken into account when interpreting its recommendations, particularly where it insists on the importance of vendors and reliable legal sources.

## 6. Perspective: towards a governance of human capacities

The article invites a more demanding conception of AI governance.

An organisation should not only ask which systems its professionals are allowed to use. It should also examine how these systems transform their intellectual practices.

This leads to distinguishing three complementary levels:

1. **Governing systems:** security, compliance, documentation, vendor control and risk management.
2. **Governing practices:** definition of uses, allocation of responsibilities, verification procedures and escalation mechanisms.
3. **Preserving and developing capacities:** understanding, critical judgement, learning, autonomy and the power to challenge.

This third dimension is essential.

It requires going beyond the usual indicators of technological transformation, such as the number of licences, the adoption rate or the time saved.

Truly human-centred governance should also seek to determine whether professionals keep their ability to justify a position, identify an error, formulate an alternative solution and exercise their judgement without assistance.

These criteria could be studied empirically through protocols comparing different ways of collaborating with AI.

The central question to ask would then be:

**How can AI governance be designed so that it does not merely prevent technological risks, but also guarantees the conditions for developing and exercising professional judgement?**

## Conclusion

Karen Waldron identifies a major difficulty in the digital transformation of the legal professions: AI systems are evolving faster than the organisational structures responsible for overseeing them.

Her analysis has the merit of putting human expertise and learning back at the centre of governance concerns.

It nevertheless calls for an extension.

Control over an artificial intelligence system cannot be assessed solely in terms of its security, its compliance or the formal responsibility of its users.

It also requires asking how this system transforms the very conditions of legal knowledge and judgement.

**The aim of governance should not simply be to keep a human in the loop, but to preserve the conditions that allow them to remain an intellectually autonomous actor within it.**
