The report *The AI-Native Law Firm* was published in June 2026 by Inside Practice, a monitoring publication that also organises conferences and maintains a directory of “77 AI-native firms and 100 vendor platforms”. It has thirteen chapters, a little under three hours of reading according to the website, and is explicitly addressed to the managing partners and boards of large law firms. The summary and the beginning of a few chapters are freely accessible: the rest sits behind a form that asks for a professional e-mail address.

It is worth reading in full because the expression “AI-native firm” is circulating more and more, often without a definition, and this report aims to provide one. It partly succeeds. It also provides a good example of how a strategy text can borrow the signs of rigour without always having its substance.

## What the report contributes: a vocabulary for taking stock

The first contribution is a three-level distinction. The “AI-using” firm has bought access to a tool (Harvey, Legora, CoCounsel) without changing anything in its workflows, its pricing or its organisation. The “AI-enabled” firm has integrated AI into the workflows of its departments and is starting to test fixed fees, but its business model remains that of a traditional firm. The “AI-native” firm is built around AI: it produces the first draft, the lawyer reviews, validates and signs, and the price no longer depends on the time spent.

The report adds a functional test, which it presents as the clearest: in a firm where AI is an add-on, removing it is an inconvenience; in an AI-native firm, removing it “breaks the operation”. The test has the merit of being simple. It also has a limit that the report does not point out: it measures a dependence, and a dependence is not in itself a sign of maturity. A firm can depend entirely on a poorly governed tool.

The definition adopted is more cautious than the title suggests. The AI-native firm is described as “lawyer-led, governance-first, data-enabled”, and the formula that accompanies it is worth remembering: “lawyer-led does not mean lawyer-only, and AI-enabled does not mean AI-unaccountable”. The report sticks to it fairly well, moreover. It reserves the scenarios of high autonomy for the forward-looking chapter and repeats that AI produces, that the lawyer judges and signs, and that governance is a commercial asset.

Three tools in the report seem to us directly usable, including by a small firm.

The first is the segmentation of pricing by type of work (chapter 7). The report rejects the idea that the billable hour will disappear and proposes keeping hourly billing for bespoke, high-stakes work, moving to fixed fees or unit pricing for repeatable work, to managed services for volume, and to subscriptions for monitoring and recurring advice. This is the most measured position in the report, and in our view the soundest.

The second is the allocation of tasks across four risk levels (chapters 4 and 6), from non-confidential internal work, which calls for a light review, up to procedural documents and investigations covered by secrecy, which call for source checking, restricted tools, traceability and a named signature. A list of prohibited uses completes the grid: unsupervised advice, automatic filing of a document, confidential data in a public tool, unchecked citation.

The third is the notion of stopping criteria (chapter 11): a pilot project is stopped or redesigned if it produces unreliable results, imposes an excessive review burden, breaches the client's instructions, is not adopted or does not improve the economics of the case file. The formula “Kill criteria preserve credibility and prevent transformation theater” sums up the idea well.

## An evidentiary apparatus that does not deliver what it announces

Chapter 13 describes an “evidentiary standard”: findings corroborated by several sources are presented as results, company figures as dated illustrations. The intention is sound. A full reading nevertheless reveals a number of internal inconsistencies that makes it necessary to take each figure with caution.

Take the size of the market. The summary and chapter 3 state that routine legal work represents 30 to 40% of corporate legal spending, then conclude: “On a $1 trillion global market, even a 10% share capture represents a $100 billion revenue opportunity.” The calculation takes 10% of the entire market and forgets the 30 to 40% that has just been announced. Chapter 9 redoes the operation correctly and arrives at “$30–40 billion”. The same report therefore gives two orders of magnitude, one three times higher than the other, and it is the higher one that appears in the summary.

The error rate of legal AI follows the same path. Chapter 3 refers to the Stanford study whose title announces hallucinations in “1 out of 6 (or More) Benchmarking Queries”. Chapters 4, 6 and 12 assert that AI gets it wrong “over 50 percent” of the time on specific legal questions, citing the same study through a Reuters article. The report does not say whether the two figures measure the same thing, and presents the second as “one of the most consequential quantitative data points”.

The time saved varies too. The summary puts forward around 240 hours per year per professional, chapter 7 and chapter 13 give 190, each time citing Thomson Reuters' *Future of Professionals* report. Grand View Research's market report gives rise to a third small discrepancy: 1.445 billion dollars in 2024 at 17.3% annual growth comes to around 3.76 billion in 2030, not the 3.918 billion announced. The gap may be due to a different base year in the source, but the report does not say so.

The methodology chapter itself is not immune. It asserts that the authorisations of Garfield AI and LawFairy are “cited from the SRA's own press releases”. The summary's note, entitled “Solicitors Regulation Authority / Garfield AI”, in fact refers to Garfield's press release, published on the company's website. In chapter 13, the note references do not match the notes: those that should refer to the SRA refer to opinion 512 of the American Bar Association and to the Stanford study. Other attributions do not hold up either: a figure attributed to Thomson Reuters comes from an Artificial Lawyer article, and a position attributed to the American Bar Association comes from a local bar association in Florida.

There remain the small assembly contradictions. Chapter 11 lists six imperatives for managing partners, then summarises “five” a few paragraphs further on. Chapter 12 promises in a subtitle “Three plausible 2030 endpoints” and develops two series of four. Garfield AI is presented as “entirely AI-driven” in one chapter, and in another as a system in which the user authorises each step and the AI cannot suggest case law. These inconsistencies do not ruin the analysis, but they suggest that the text was assembled from several versions without an overall review.

The most important problem lies elsewhere. The case studies that carry the argument rest almost entirely on sources controlled by the companies: press releases (Harvey, Legora, Freshfields, Garfield), investor blogs (Sequoia), fundraising aggregators. Crosby's figures (13,000 contracts, a median turnaround of 58 minutes, 400% growth) come back in five chapters, always from a fundraising profile. Chapter 13 honestly acknowledges this: these are figures “disclosed or reported” and not verified. The report should therefore be read as a synthesis of what the market says about itself, and not as a measure of what it does.

We add a piece of context that the report does not mention: its publisher sells events and visibility in a directory to software vendors and investors, which it itself presents as a target audience (“Vendors & Investors”). We do not infer from this that the conclusions are biased. We note that no declaration of interests accompanies a text whose recommendations (investing in infrastructure, overhauling pricing, watching new entrants) align with the interests of this audience.

## What the report does not see: continental Europe

The report is built entirely on two markets, the United States and England. Across the chapters devoted to the market, organisation, technology, governance, staff, practice areas, clients, the roadmap and the scenarios, the GDPR does not appear, nor do Belgium or France, and professional secrecy is never named as such. The European regulation on artificial intelligence is mentioned in one chapter, in passing, within a list of frameworks. Secrecy is dealt with exclusively from the angle of American “privilege”, opinion 512 of the American Bar Association and a New York federal decision.

This absence is not a detail, because the model the report describes depends on it. The report says so itself in chapter 12: “The decisive constraint is structural rather than ethical”. In most American states, Rule 5.4 of the American Bar Association's model rules prohibits the ownership of a law firm by non-lawyers, and the report explains that the AI-native firms it cites were able to raise funds thanks to the exceptions of Arizona and the English Legal Services Act 2007. Norm Law is backed by Blackstone, Bain Capital and Vanguard; Crosby by Sequoia. Chapter 7 adopts Bloomberg Law's thesis that the most direct route to the AI-native firm runs through outside capital.

The Belgian reader must therefore ask a question that the report does not ask: what remains of the model when this capital is not available? We do not answer it here. It requires examining the Belgian rules of professional ethics on practising through a company and on the ownership of capital, with the texts to hand, and will be the subject of a separate note in Perspectives.

## What a Belgian law firm can take from it

Read with these reservations, the report remains useful, provided it is taken for what it is: an interpretive grid for taking stock, not a demonstration.

A firm of five to twenty lawyers has no pyramid of associates to protect and no budget of 500 million dollars to commit, and most of the report's recommendations (a steering committee with an IT security officer, a competitive intelligence function, a vectorised repository of the firm's entire history) do not concern it. Three elements, on the other hand, carry over effortlessly: classifying one's services by type of work before choosing a pricing method, writing a rule for allocating tasks by risk level, and setting in advance the criteria that will stop a trial.

The first ties in with the standardisation and stakes matrix proposed in the “Automating a service” section, which adds to the report's segmentation a variable it ignores: what is at stake in the case for the client. The second corresponds to what the [AI Governance](/outils/gouvernance-ia/) toolkit calls the approval of uses and supervision. The third requires only one written page before the first trial.

We are preparing a self-assessment grid that takes up these three elements at the scale of a small firm, and a note on the AI-native law firm under Belgian law. The [review of Olivier Chaduteau's book](/revue/recensions/impact-ia-juristes-avocats-affaires-chaduteau/), which deals with the same transformations of the business model from the perspective of the French market, is a good complement.
