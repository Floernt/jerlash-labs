The language used to describe artificial intelligence shapes the way its users picture its capabilities and grant it their trust. In an exchange published by *EDHEC Vox*, Arnaud Billion and Björn Fasterling examine three expressions that have become familiar: “bias”, “companion” and “hallucinations”. Their conversation invites us to be wary of words that implicitly attribute human faculties or intentions to a technical system. It also opens a difficulty: how can these metaphors be criticised without giving up the technical and legal distinctions they can usefully designate?

## The argument: three words, three shifts

The text reproduces an exchange that took place at the Summer School organised in early September 2026 by the EDHEC Augmented Law Institute. It is a conceptual discussion, informed by scientific references and by considerations on the uses of AI. It presents neither a user survey nor experiments on models. Its guiding question is the effect of words on our understanding of systems and, consequently, on the way we act with them.

The first discussion concerns **bias** (section “Quand on affirme que l’IA est “biaisée””). Arnaud Billion challenges the self-evidence of the term: declaring a system “biased” presupposes a reference state against which the deviation is measured. Yet that point of comparison may itself depend on conventional choices. Regarding automated recruitment, he questions the claim to define a “normal” or neutral selection. His criticism goes as far as doubting the general usefulness of the notion when it conceals the choices that structure any classification.

Björn Fasterling introduces a distinction. Technical bias refers to a deviation assessed against a defined reference, whereas unjustified discrimination involves a moral norm. Confusing these levels leads both to reading a statistical property as an injustice and to presenting an injustice as a mere calculation defect. The two speakers therefore share a concern about vocabulary without giving it exactly the same scope: Billion rejects the framing in terms of “bias” more broadly; Fasterling mainly seeks to clarify its uses.

The **companion** is the second term discussed (section “Que se cache-t-il derrière l’idée d’un “compagnon d’IA” ?”). Billion criticises the image of an informed, loyal and available entity that would spontaneously defend its user’s interests. He points to the economic organisations that design and market these services. Fasterling stresses what the unified interface makes invisible: a distributed infrastructure combining machines, programs, access rights and institutional arrangements. The seemingly personal relationship with an assistant thus tends to obscure the material and organisational conditions of the answer.

The last discussion concerns **hallucinations** (section “Peut-on vraiment dire que l’IA “hallucine” ?”). The authors underline the gap between the ordinary meaning of the word, which presupposes a lived experience, and the functioning of a generative system. Billion notes that a manifestly false answer may result from a technical execution that conforms to the intended calculations. Fasterling stresses that speaking of hallucination risks attributing to the system an experience that nothing in the text allows us to grant it. Their exchange ends on the term *prompt*, which Fasterling likens to the prompter in a theatre: the user gives a cue, while a computing infrastructure produces the performance.

## Discussion: how sound is a critique of vocabulary?

The most convincing contribution of this conversation lies in separating registers. Describing a classification operation, judging whether it is fair and attributing an intention to the tool are different acts. Confusing them makes it harder to identify the human choices that organise the production and use of answers. This distinction is useful in professions where the way a problem is framed already determines part of how it is handled.

The passage on bias nevertheless calls for a reservation. The fact that a statistical or normative reference results from a choice does not necessarily make the measurement useless. A system can be compared with a defined population, a decision rule or an explicit non-discrimination requirement. The problem is then not the existence of the word “bias”, but the lack of information on the chosen reference, its legitimacy and the consequences of the deviation observed. The distinction introduced by Fasterling precisely keeps this possibility of analysis open. In law, moreover, the relevance of a selection criterion and the justification of a difference in treatment are not settled merely by observing that an algorithm distinguishes between files.

The analysis of the “companion” is relevant for understanding the role of the interface. A continuous dialogue, a proper name or a personalised answer can give an appearance of coherence to a much larger technical and commercial assembly. On the other hand, reducing AI to “a piece of code”, as Billion does in the section on the companion, risks producing a simplification symmetrical to the one he denounces. Models, their training, their integration into services and the rules governing their operation cannot be understood through that description alone. Fasterling’s attention to infrastructure offers a more precise way of examining responsibilities and dependencies.

As for “hallucinations”, the discussion rightly distinguishes an error of content from an imaginary perceptual experience. The operational usefulness of the term should nevertheless be preserved when it refers, by convention, to the production of unfounded or incorrect information. An output can be produced without any execution anomaly and still fail with regard to the task requested. In legal matters this difference has another dimension: a non-existent case reference is not made acceptable by the correctness of the calculation that generated it. Criticising anthropomorphism therefore does not dispense us from building verifiable categories of error.

Finally, the hypotheses concerning commercial interests and the potential effects of profiling are relevant for guiding inquiry, but the conversation offers no method for assessing their frequency or effect in the services actually used. The scope of the text remains argumentative and exploratory. It is on that scale that it should be assessed.

## Why it matters for lawyers

The conversation encourages lawyers to specify what they mean when they attribute to an AI a capacity to understand, advise or decide. This requirement applies as much to exchanges with clients as to internal governance documents and to instructions given to professionals. A convenient label can become misleading when it avoids describing the function actually performed, the criteria applied or the actor who answers for the result.

The article thus recalls a useful requirement for human oversight: choosing words that make it possible to describe what the machine does, what the user expects of it and what remains to be checked. Terminological precision does not by itself ensure the professional’s autonomy. It helps make the conditions under which that autonomy is exercised open to discussion.
