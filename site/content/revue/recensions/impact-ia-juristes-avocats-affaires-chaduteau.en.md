Artificial intelligence is not simply a new tool available to legal professionals. It changes the conditions in which legal services are produced, distributed and valued. This is the conviction running through Olivier Chaduteau’s book, *L’impact de l’IA sur les juristes et les avocats d’affaires*.

The interest of this contribution lies in the paradigm it proposes: rather than asking only what artificial intelligence makes it possible to automate, the author considers the structural transformations it imposes on legal organisations.

But this economic and organisational reading opens up a complementary question: **if artificial intelligence transforms the conditions in which law is practised, how does it transform the conditions in which lawyers acquire, exercise and retain their capacity for judgement?**

## A systemic transformation of the legal market

Olivier Chaduteau approaches artificial intelligence as a general purpose technology, a notion developed in particular by Bresnahan and Trajtenberg (p. 23). Its spread therefore goes beyond the occasional improvement of certain processes: it is likely to reshape an entire economic activity.

Applied to the legal professions, this perspective leads beyond the question of automation alone. The issue becomes the reshaping of the value chain of production around law.

The author considers in particular the transformation of law firms’ business models, changing client expectations, the development of new services and the creation of hybrid organisations combining legal professionals and artificial intelligence systems (pp. 70-71).

This approach invites legal professionals to stop regarding AI as a mere productivity instrument and to see it instead as a factor of strategic transformation.

A law firm would thus no longer be merely an organisation bringing together human skills, but an organised set of human and technical resources, whose combination would determine its capacity to produce value.

This analysis is particularly relevant for a profession whose business model remains largely structured around billing working time.

If a growing share of legal production can be carried out more quickly, the relationship between time spent, price charged and value delivered necessarily becomes problematic.

## From legal production to anticipating needs

One of the most interesting developments concerns the shift of value towards knowledge of the client and the anticipation of their needs.

The author sketches the move from an essentially reactive service towards a more proactive legal relationship.

The author takes the example of professional communication: traditional newsletters could gradually give way to personalised, contextualised alerts accompanied by proposals for concrete action (pp. 73-75).

The value of advice would then lie less in passing on legal information than in contextualising it, linking it to the client’s activities and translating it into possible decisions.

This development can also be understood as a shift of legal services towards a form of continuous support.

Lawyers would no longer merely answer a question put by their client. They would help identify questions that have not yet arisen for the client.

Artificial intelligence could thus encourage the move from an economy of answers to an economy of anticipation.

This perspective nevertheless raises a difficulty: multiplying the capacity for detection and recommendation does not, by itself, guarantee that interventions are relevant.

Anticipating does not simply mean predicting. One must still assess what a situation means, rank what is at stake and decide whether an intervention is justified.

In other words, **the technical capacity to produce recommendations is not the same as the faculty of judging whether they are appropriate**.

## The paradox of legal competence

The reflection becomes more delicate when the author turns to the evolution of intellectual work.

He considers in particular a shift in the role of professionals, from research, collection and analysis towards control, analysis and anticipation (p. 152).

This redistribution seems consistent with the development of systems capable of carrying out research, summarising documents or preparing legal reasoning.

It nevertheless raises a paradox.

To check an output effectively, one must have the skills needed to understand it, assess it and, where appropriate, challenge it.

Yet these skills have historically been built through practising the very operations that it is now proposed to delegate. The question is especially pressing for young lawyers.

How can they develop their analytical capacity if a growing part of their learning, previously based on research, reading, comparing sources and drafting, is now handled by automated systems?

The risk does not necessarily lie in an immediate loss of competence, but in a gradual transformation of the conditions in which it is acquired.

## Writing, thinking and human oversight

A particularly stimulating passage concerns the place of writing.

Olivier Chaduteau observes that voice interfaces could save considerable time, while stressing the risk of weakened reflection when users do not keep sufficient critical distance. He also recalls that writing is an important means of structuring thought (pp. 25-26).

Legal writing is not a mere transcription of reasoning formed beforehand. In some cases, it contributes to forming it.

Drafting an opinion, building an argument or reformulating a difficulty forces lawyers to make relationships explicit, test hypotheses against each other and organise elements that are sometimes contradictory.

Producing the document and producing the thought are therefore not two entirely separable activities. When artificial intelligence takes over part of this drafting activity, it does not only change the speed at which the document is produced. It potentially intervenes in the very process by which the reasoning is formed.

This does not mean that using AI necessarily leads to diminished intellectual capacities.

A critical interaction with a generative system can also encourage the exploration of hypotheses, the confrontation of arguments and the explicit statement of reasoning.

It all depends on how this interaction takes place.

This observation leads beyond an essentially procedural conception of human oversight. Checking an answer, approving a document or formally retaining the final decision is not always enough to guarantee the exercise of independent judgement.

One must also ask whether the professional actually retains the means to understand the proposal, challenge it and formulate an alternative solution.

Human oversight cannot therefore be reduced to an approval step in an automated process. It must also be conceived as a condition for maintaining and developing the capacity for judgement.

## Beyond efficiency, what conception of the lawyer?

Olivier Chaduteau’s book makes a useful contribution to understanding the economic and organisational transformations of the legal professions.

Its value lies in showing that the adoption of artificial intelligence cannot be considered independently of organisations’ strategy, their business models and their relationship with clients.

It also invites us to see lawyers as actors of innovation, in particular through their upstream involvement in the design of products and services (pp. 61-63).

The perspective nevertheless deserves to be taken further.

A successful transformation should not be measured solely by a legal organisation’s capacity to produce more, faster or in a more personalised way. It should also be assessed in the light of its members’ capacity to develop their discernment, to retain intellectual command of their activity and to take an effective part in the technical choices that are reshaping their profession.

The lawyer of tomorrow will probably be neither the one who refuses artificial intelligence systems nor the one who delegates their activity to them indiscriminately.

It could be the one who knows how to build a demanding relationship with these systems, in which technology increases the possibilities for action without impoverishing the capacities for understanding and judgement.

Chaduteau’s book thus opens a discussion that goes beyond the legal professions alone: the conditions under which an organisation can become technologically more efficient without making its members intellectually more dependent.
