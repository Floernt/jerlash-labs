The first episode of the podcast *L'IA juridique en pratique* (“Legal AI in practice”) brings together Hugo Salard, a lawyer at the Paris Bar, and Pierre Colliot, founder of Zevra, a company that develops tailor-made AI tools for law firms. The conversation covers a lot of ground: the models used, the market for legal tools, confidentiality, the future of the profession. We will retain one thesis, because it comes up regularly in law firms, whatever their size: the documentary corpus a firm needs lies, for the most part, in its own case files. Law firms are sitting on a (gold) mine they do not exploit.

The quotations below are our own translation of our transcription of the recording.

## The argument: from the firm's written submissions to its own tools

Pierre Colliot starts from an observation made in the course of the audits his company carries out. In a firm with twenty or thirty years of history, what the lawyer uses day to day is no longer at the publisher's: the publishers' corpus is “distilled into the submissions”, into the lawyer's written pleadings. The publisher is still consulted for an update or a scholarly article; the rest is already in the files. He draws the consequence: “the lawyer has the capacity to produce his own doctrine and his own corpus”, provided the data are properly organised and processed. Open data adds the layer of novelty, for instance a published judgment that may call into question what the firm had been arguing until then.

The example that carries the argument is Dairia, a Lyon firm specialising in employment law that advises employers. Its founder indexed ten years of submissions and opinions in a database that AI can query. The service is made available to clients' HR departments: a question receives an answer based on the firm's reasoning and, if it is too complex, it is passed on to a lawyer. Pierre Colliot adds that a ten-person firm can today produce what used to take a much larger structure.

The rest of the episode develops this idea. The general tools on the market (Harvey, Legora and the publishers' offers) are judged expensive compared with a subscription to a general-purpose model. The guest advocates building one's own tools, on models that can be changed or hosted in-house, so as not to become captive to a provider.

## Discussion: the mine exists, exploiting it has conditions

In our view, the thesis is sound, and it matters more than the figures around it. A firm accumulates, case after case, legal characterisations, strategies, wordings that convinced or failed. This knowledge is today scattered across folders and in the partners' memory. Making it searchable is feasible without considerable means, and it is the use of AI that creates the most value specific to the firm, whereas access to a general tool only creates value that all competitors share.

The ore still has to be sorted. We see three conditions that the episode touches on.

The first concerns the quality of the corpus. Submissions are adversarial writings, drafted for a court at a given date. Some relate to cases that were lost; others rest on outdated case law. A database that returns them without this information reproduces the firm's mistakes with the authority of an instant answer. Pierre Colliot sees this when he mentions the published judgment that changes the picture; it is this work of sorting, dating and updating that turns an archive into a corpus, and it cannot be fully automated.

The second concerns professional secrecy. A firm's written submissions contain its clients' facts. Reusing these documents to answer other clients, or sending them to a model hosted by a third party, raises a question that the episode mainly addresses from a technical angle (pseudonymisation, local hosting). The guest himself acknowledges that pseudonymisation has a limit: a person remains identifiable through context.

The third concerns the lawyer's role. Dairia's service answers legal questions put by clients and only passes on to a lawyer what appears complex. Who decides what is complex, and who answers for the answer given when nobody has reviewed it? The same question applies to mass litigation, where the lawyer is only alerted if a document “falls outside the frame” in the example given. We are not saying here that these systems are at fault: they can be well designed. But their value depends on how the lawyer's responsibility is organised within them, and the episode does not discuss it.

## Why it matters for lawyers

For a lawyer, the practical lesson is simple and can start without a developer: take stock of what the firm has already produced in an area it frequently practises, sort it (what was won, what is dated, what is confidential…), and turn it into an internal working base before considering a service open to clients.
