Litil describes itself as a “legal AI laboratory” developing open models for the analysis of legal documents. Its team includes several lawyer-developers. The note reviewed, published on 5 November 2026 without an author's name, recounts a precise technical experiment: how the laboratory improved its model for locating contract clauses by averaging three training runs. The text is written for engineers. We review it because it does something rare for a commercial player: it publishes its tables, including those that do not suit it, which makes it possible to discuss it on the evidence.

## What the note says

The technique described is called “model soup”. It was proposed by Wortsman et al. in 2022. The same model is trained several times, with the same data and the same settings, changing only the random seed (the parameter that fixes the randomness of training). The weights obtained are then averaged. The result is a single model, which costs nothing more to use.

Litil applies the technique to a small open model, Qwen3-1.7B, adapted to two tasks: finding in a contract the passage that answers a question, and answering closed-choice questions. The data are public datasets (CUAD, MAUD, ContractNLI) supplemented by tasks generated by the laboratory.

The account has three stages. The first training run fails one of the five checks set in advance: it scores 0.612 on a measure for which the threshold was 0.80. Two other runs, with other seeds, score 0.878 and 0.952. The three are merged, including the one that had failed. The merged model passes all five checks and becomes the laboratory's new model.

The result put forward: on 320 contract-clause pairs, the right passage comes first in 92.2% of cases, against 77.8% for the previous model, and the number of false alerts per contract falls from 49.8 to 12.2.

The note also contains a mathematical development on the right way to merge LoRA adapters (a lightweight training method) and a warning about calibration: recalibrating a model on its own training data amounts, according to the authors, to asking the cook whether dinner was good.

## Discussion

### The gain comes mainly from retraining

The figure of 77.8% to 92.2% compares the merged model with the previous model. The note's tables show that each of the three runs, taken in isolation, already does almost as well: 93.4%, 90.9% and 91.3%. The first run, the one that had been set aside, even does better than the merge on this measure.

The fifteen-point jump therefore comes from the new training recipe, not from the merge. The merge brings something else, which the note demonstrates better: it smooths the results and makes it possible to pass all the checks without having to choose the “right” run. The subtitle, which attributes the gain to the soup, seems to us to overstate the technique at the expense of the work that preceded it.

### What the tables qualify

Several published data points are not commented on in the text. On the measure where the first run had failed, the merged model scores 0.878, exactly the score of one of the three runs, and slightly less than the previous model (0.894, which appears on the chart without appearing in the table). On the rate of answers found, the merged model (92.5%) does worse than the previous model (94.4%).

The test set for this measure has 51 questions, of which four are positive cases. The published confidence intervals are very wide, from 0.766 to 0.963 for the merged model, and the note itself states that they do not reflect variation between runs. With four positive cases, a single misclassified case shifts the score significantly.

Finally, the calibration comparison sets a value “reconstructed offline” for the merged model against values measured directly for the three runs. The note flags this in its table, but the sentence “three times better” does not recall it.

### The rule of checks set in advance

We retain a methodological practice worth adopting: the laboratory writes its checks before each training run and forbids itself from changing them afterwards. It is the equivalent, for a model, of pre-registering a research protocol. The note does not list them all and brings back into the merge the run that had failed one of them. We are not writing here that this is a mistake: the technique rests precisely on averaging several runs. But the rule does not prevent relaunching runs until a result passes, and the note does not explain how the number three was set.

### The status of the text

The note is published by a company offering its services (“Work with us”, “Book a call”) and presents its own model, without naming it. The earlier trials mentioned at the end of the note are not quantified and the October data are not published. It is an account of an internal experiment, not a peer-reviewed study. This status takes nothing away from the transparency of the tables, which is real and which we would like to see more often.

## Relevance for legal professionals

For a lawyer, the note has no direct use: model merging is done upstream, by whoever trains the model. It has an indirect one, important in our view. It shows from the inside that, with the same data and the same recipe, a simple change of randomness moves a model from “rejected” to “accepted” on a quality criterion. The performance of a contract analysis tool is therefore not a stable property that is measured once. It depends on training choices that the user does not see.

The practical consequence, for a firm evaluating a tool, is to ask the provider on which test set its figures were measured, how many positive cases it contains and whether the results were reproduced over several training runs. Most providers do not publish this information; Litil, here, does.

For the jerlash.labs AI & Trust programme, the note provides a concrete case: a headline figure that is accurate but attributed to the wrong cause, and tables that allow the attentive reader to correct it.
