## Resistance to innovation that goes beyond technology

Why do some innovations, even when they promise to improve professional practice significantly, meet persistent resistance from those who should be their first beneficiaries?

In his article of 2 November 2026, Richard Tromans offers an answer: the obstacle to the adoption of artificial intelligence may be less technological than a matter of identity.

Drawing on a talk given by Malcolm Gladwell at ClioCon 2026, Tromans puts forward a hypothesis: adopting an innovation does not simply mean changing one's working methods. It may require professionals to transform the way they see their own role.

He takes up two examples mentioned by Gladwell: less invasive surgical techniques, which involve tools and greater cooperation, and policing strategies based on prevention and community intervention. In both cases, according to the interpretation reported in the article, the innovation touches the symbolic foundations of professional legitimacy: the surgeon would no longer be the sole master of the technical gesture, and the police officer would no longer identify exclusively with law enforcement.

These are **analogies reported by Tromans**, not empirical demonstrations establishing that legal AI will produce the same effects.

## The traditional identity of the partner called into question

In the portrait Tromans draws, the identity of the equity partner traditionally rests on several functions: attracting and retaining clients, generating revenue, managing associates, organising delegation and guaranteeing the quality of the legal work.

These functions are at once economic, organisational and symbolic. They help build a position of authority within the firm.

Intensive integration of AI could alter this balance. Associates perform part of their tasks with generative systems. Partners can carry out directly, at their own desk, tasks they would previously have delegated. Clients also have tools that allow them to deal with certain questions without systematically consulting their lawyer.

According to Tromans, the partner keeps their commercial role, the client relationship and final responsibility, but becomes more of a process planner, an arbiter of the division between human work and automation, and an organiser of quality control. Billing and internal training could also change.

The issue is therefore not only one of acquiring an instrumental skill: it touches the activities in which professionals recognise their own expertise.

## A relevant but incomplete explanation

The main interest of the article lies in shifting the question from technology to professional identity and the evolution of the role. An innovation perceived as useful can give rise to resistance that training in the tools alone cannot resolve.

This explanation nevertheless calls for three reservations.

### Resistance is not necessarily psychological

Professional identity has an institutional and material dimension. The partner's authority is tied to a business model, to responsibilities, to the division of work and to the passing on of skills. Changing these mechanisms can give rise to rational objections, independently of any attachment to professional prestige.

Refusing an AI system may also be justified when its reliability, confidentiality, relevance or liability model are not sufficiently established.

### Adoption is not an end in itself

The analogies with surgery and policing do not show that all legal automation improves services. Each use should be assessed on the basis of its actual consequences for the quality of the service, for professionals, clients and institutions.

Technical transformation does not automatically amount to professional progress.

### The question goes beyond partners alone

When tasks traditionally entrusted to junior associates are automated, the conditions of their learning change. How can professional judgement be developed when some of the exercises that formed it disappear or are profoundly mediated by technical systems?

The transformation of professional identity therefore also concerns associates, clients and the organisation of firms.

## A new distribution of professional authority?

Tromans suggests that the partner's authority could be partly diluted by AI. But a reduced dependence on a partner does not necessarily translate into a more equal distribution of power.

Authority can shift towards the people who design processes, choose tools, define data or control technical infrastructure. It can also become concentrated with system vendors.

It is therefore important to distinguish **three transformations**, which do not necessarily go in the same direction:

1. **Redistribution of tasks**: who does the work?
2. **Redistribution of knowledge**: who understands, knows how to check and can learn?
3. **Redistribution of decision-making power**: who sets the objectives, arbitrates and can challenge?

A lawyer can become more productive without strengthening their epistemic autonomy. A client can receive more information without being better able to assess risks. An associate can produce faster without acquiring the skills they will need to exercise independent judgement.

## Collectively rebuilding the conditions of judgement

The decisive question is to determine **which transformations are desirable, for whom and under what conditions**.

A legal organisation cannot be content with keeping a human as the final validator of a largely automated process. It must preserve the concrete means to understand, check, correct, challenge and justify decisions.

AI does not only raise the question of what lawyers will do tomorrow. It questions how they will be able to keep building their skills and exercising their judgement in a profoundly transformed technical environment.
