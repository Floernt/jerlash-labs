The law firm Dentons published in October 2026, in its *Leading Edge* series, a 31-page report entitled *From AI Adoption to AI Advantage*. It is signed by two partners of the Dublin office, Carlo Salizzo and David Kirton. The executive summary is signed by Ann O'Dea, CEO and editor-in-chief of Silicon Republic, an Irish technology media outlet.

It describes the use of AI on the clients' side, in-house lawyers, whereas most of the texts we review here are about law firms. And it comes from a European Union country, where the GDPR applies, which makes it closer to Belgian practice than American or English reports.

## The report

The report rests on “in-depth interviews” with legal and compliance leaders of companies among which it cites seven organisations (eir, Flipdish, Gilead Sciences, IMRO, Mastercard, Uniphar and Wayflyer; the list is introduced by “including”), supplemented by the words of three people from Dentons, the two partners already mentioned and Cornelius Grossmann, Global Head of Solutions Development. Nine people from outside the firm are quoted by name. The executive summary identifies five themes, which the five chapters develop.

The first chapter describes AI as an assistance layer rather than a replacement. The report makes this its clearest conclusion: the replacement of a role or function is “very much the exception, not the rule”. The uses the interviewees are proud of concern narrow, well-scoped tasks refined several times. At Uniphar, Sergey Kinchin wrote a “final checker” agent that reviews a contract before signature without looking for legal issues, only cross-references, spelling and consistency. He also asked a Microsoft agentic tool to go through nine months of his emails to trace, clause by clause, what had been negotiated in each master services agreement. At eir, the legal team only started talking about AI after years of gathering its documents in a single repository.

The second chapter deals with adoption and governance. The report observes that AI governance is leaving the legal department to become the business of several functions. At eir, each new use goes before an AI governance forum on which two lawyers and the data protection officer sit, and must then be approved after review by this forum, IT security, legal and data protection. At IMRO, the Copilot rollout only worked once it was turned into a five-week programme, with workshops, drop-in sessions and documentation pages.

The third chapter is, in our view, the most interesting. It describes what the report calls the volume problem: complaints, data subject access requests, employee grievances and customer letters drafted with AI arrive in greater numbers, longer and more legal in form. Sorcha Nic Mhurchú, General Counsel of eir, places the cost in the right place: the work does not consist in reading, but in working out, “line by line, what is relevant”. The phenomenon also exists inside the company: Sergey Kinchin recounts a meeting where five people brought six AI-generated opinions on a regulatory question.

The fourth chapter deals with costs. It announces the shift from licence-based pricing to consumption-based pricing (“tokens”), and the difficulty of measuring a return on investment. The fifth reports what in-house lawyers expect from law firms: that they use AI, responsibly, and pass on part of the gains. Views on the hourly rate are divided.

## What is established, what is reported, what is projected

The report does not claim to be a survey. The foreword explains that it was written in reaction to “countless surveys with convincing statistics” and that it seeks to find out what is really happening, beyond the marketing materials of law firms and vendors. It is a qualitative approach, and it must be read as such. Three limits follow.

The method is not described. We know neither the full list of organisations, nor the number of interviews, nor their dates, nor the questions asked, nor how the organisations were chosen, except that they are “some of the most innovative legal teams in the market”. The only indication of date is the note “All roles and organisations correct at the time of interview”. The sample is therefore chosen for being ahead, it is mainly Irish, and it allows no generalisation. The report is careful about this in the body of the text, much less so in its executive summary and conclusion.

That is where the second problem lies. Several statements in the executive summary and conclusion have no support in the chapters.

Let us take the cost of AI. The executive summary attributes to Cornelius Grossmann the expectation that AI costs will rise “two- to fivefold within two years”. In the chapter on costs, Grossmann speaks of a coming “cost cliff” and says that token economics “could bankrupt you if you don't pay attention”, but no figure appears. The conclusion, for its part, reduces the point to that of “one interviewee” who “went so far as to warn of a coming cost cliff”. The same warning is thus presented as a quantified forecast in the executive summary and as an isolated opinion in the conclusion. This figure of two to five times is a projection by a partner of the firm publishing the report, and nothing more.

The warning also deserves to be put in perspective with the models used, since the cost of use depends first on the model to which the task is entrusted. So-called “frontier” models, the most recent and most capable of each provider, are also the most expensive per token. Earlier versions cost significantly less and, given the progression in performance from one generation to the next, they are often enough for a well-defined task. Let us take Uniphar's final checker agent: checking the cross-references, spelling and consistency of a contract does not require the latest model available. In our view, cost control therefore lies in choosing the model task by task, rather than using the most powerful model for everything. The report does not consider this avenue. Another development points in the same direction: machines capable of running open-source models locally are becoming more affordable. The cost then shifts to hardware and electricity, and consumption can be tracked more finely than with a per-token bill. The reasoning remains the same: which model, for which task, with what level of performance.

The executive summary also states that AI-assisted drafting “now seems to touch the majority of incoming disputes, subject access requests, grievances and customer complaints”. The corresponding chapter contains no count; it reports converging testimony, which is something other than a measured majority.

The third problem lies in the firm's position. Dentons is at once the investigator, some of the interviewees and the beneficiary of certain conclusions. Its partners' words close almost every chapter. The chapter on volume ends with the announcement that Dentons now offers its clients a managed service for high-volume complaints, and the chapter on the relationship with law firms with the firm's wish to support its clients “on the AI journey”. We do not infer that the testimony is false. We note that the selection of quotes and the order of the chapters also serve to present the firm's offering, which the report does not openly flag.

## What the report does not address

For an Irish report on the use of AI by lawyers, two absences are surprising. The confidentiality of data sent to the tools is never discussed. Wayflyer runs the same task through “two or three tools” to compare results; Uniphar has nine months of emails analysed, which contain exchanges with counterparties. The report describes these uses with enthusiasm without saying which data go where, or on what basis. The European regulation on artificial intelligence is not mentioned either; the only regulatory trace is the presence of the data protection officer on eir's governance forum. Professional secrecy appears only once, in the foreword, in the form of common-law “legal privilege”.

## What a European lawyer can take from it

Read as a collection of testimony, the report remains useful, more so than many quantified surveys. Three observations seem to us valid beyond Ireland.

The first concerns measurement. The foreword makes a fair point: lawyers record “the things we do, not the things we no longer have to do”, so time saved is hard to demonstrate against a cost that, for its part, is easy to measure. Rebekah Dobson (Gilead Sciences) draws the consequence: nobody has demonstrated a return on investment for lack of having measured the starting point. Jessica Williams (Mastercard) adds a point that contradicts the usual discourse: a task done in two hours without AI sometimes takes three with it, and one must ask what risk AI reduced, but also “what risk did you introduce?”. A firm considering a tool can take the lesson simply: record the time and the result of a few tasks before the trial.

The second concerns volume. Chapter 3 describes a shift in cost that the law has not anticipated: AI reduces the cost of producing a request, not the cost of the response, and the recipient often remains bound to reply point by point. For a lawyer, the question arises first of all for access requests under the GDPR. The report describes the phenomenon without examining the rules governing the response; we take up this point in a working paper.

The third concerns training. Cornelius Grossmann describes the risk of a “judgement gap”: AI pays off most when used by experienced lawyers who spot its errors, and younger lawyers risk learning “too fast” without acquiring that judgement. This is the question raised by the [Agency & AI](/recherche/autonomie-ia/) research programme, and the report has the merit of having it come from a practitioner rather than a researcher.

The report's conclusion fits into one sentence that we gladly take up: clients expect firms to be “able to show, credibly, how a human being reviewed the output”. The report does not say how. The working paper accompanying this review examines what such a demonstration might be, along with the volume problem and that of the cost of use.
