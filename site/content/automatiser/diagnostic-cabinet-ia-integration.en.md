The report *The AI-Native Law Firm* by Inside Practice (June 2026) distinguishes three levels of firm: the one that uses AI, the one that has integrated it into its workflows, and the one that is built around it. The distinction is useful, but the report applies it to American and English firms of several hundred lawyers, and its maturity model gives no verifiable criteria. We wrote a [critical review](/revue/recensions/cabinet-avocats-ai-native-inside-practice/) of it.

The grid below takes up the idea of three levels, but describes it through signs that a firm of five to twenty lawyers can observe in-house in an hour. It does not reproduce the report's model: the dimensions and criteria are jerlash.labs' own.

One precaution before starting. The highest level is not a goal in itself. A firm whose practice consists of unique, high-stakes, hardly repeatable case files may well stop at the second level, and that is a reasonable decision. The grid serves to know where one stands, and to check that the dimensions move forward at the same pace.

## The three levels

**Uses.** Lawyers use AI tools, with or without the firm's authorisation. Use depends on each individual. Nothing has changed in the organisation of work, pricing or training.

**Integrates.** The firm has chosen its tools, set rules and defined, for certain tasks, a way of working with AI. The business model remains the hourly one, but a few services are offered differently.

**Built around.** For an identified part of its services, the firm has redesigned the work: AI produces the first draft according to a written procedure, the lawyer checks, decides and signs, and the price no longer depends on the time spent. Removing AI would stop these services.

## The grid

| Dimension | Uses | Integrates | Built around |
|---|---|---|---|
| Workflow | Everyone uses AI in their own way, case by case. | For a few tasks (summary, first draft of a type of document), a written procedure says which tool to use and how. | For identified services, the case file first goes through a tool-supported procedure: the lawyer acts on what the procedure flags. |
| Supervision | Checking depends on each person's vigilance. | A written rule classifies tasks by risk level and sets the expected check for each. | Checking is built into the procedure (checklist, named sign-off), and errors found are recorded. |
| Knowledge | Templates and precedents sit in personal folders or in the partners' memory. | A library of approved templates exists and is kept up to date by a designated person. | Each closed case file feeds the library (clause retained, negotiated deviation, corrected error), according to a procedure. |
| Pricing | Everything is billed by the hour: time saved thanks to AI is lost to the firm or passed on to the client without saying so. | A few repeatable services are offered at a fixed fee, based on a known cost price. | Redesigned services have a fixed price or a subscription, and their margin is tracked case file by case file. |
| Governance | No written rule, or a rule nobody consults. | A usage policy, a list of authorised tools, information for clients in the terms of engagement. | Uses are inventoried, approved and reviewed on a fixed date: an incident has a procedure. |
| Training | Everyone learns alone. | Shared training on the tools and on checking, for everyone, including staff. | Trainee lawyers learn to check and correct a first draft, with exercises on typical errors: whether judgement is forming is measured. |

## How to use it

For each dimension, choose the column that describes what actually happens today, not what is planned or wished for. A document that exists but that nobody applies places the dimension in the left-hand column.

The useful result is not a score. It is the profile. Let us take an example: a firm that places itself at "built around" for workflow, but at "uses" for supervision and governance, has built fast production without control. This is the riskiest situation in the grid, riskier than staying in the first column everywhere. Conversely, a firm advanced in governance and lagging on pricing bears the cost of control without drawing any economic benefit from it, and that is the signal to look at its prices.

Three reading rules seem to us sufficient.

Supervision and governance should never lag behind workflow. Before moving one column forward in the way of producing, one must have moved one column forward in the way of checking.

Pricing follows workflow, not the other way round. Offering a fixed fee for a service that has no procedure amounts to betting on the time it will take.

Training conditions everything else in the medium term. A firm that advances everywhere except there risks producing lawyers able to review a first draft without being able to write it.
