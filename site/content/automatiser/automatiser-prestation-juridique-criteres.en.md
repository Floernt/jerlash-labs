Every week, an entrepreneurial lawyer asks me a variant of the same question: “Can I automate this?” The short answer is: sometimes yes, sometimes no, and the distinction is not always where you think it is.

Before setting out to automate a service, there are four variables to examine. They do not give a definitive answer, but they help avoid two symmetrical mistakes: automating what should not be automated, and not automating what could be automated without risk.

## The complexity perceived by the client

What matters here is not the actual complexity of the service, it is the complexity felt by someone who does not know the subject. A shareholders’ agreement is objectively complex. But a founder who has never negotiated one may think that ChatGPT will be enough for them. Conversely, a formal notice looks simple, but a client in a hurry will prefer to delegate it rather than fumble around.

The question to ask: if this client tries to do the thing themselves with GenAI, will they get lost? If so, automation can go a long way without devaluing the service. If not, the automated product must be visibly better than what the client would produce alone, otherwise they will not see the added value.

## The financial or patrimonial stakes

For the recovery of a 1,000-euro debt, the client will not pay a speed premium. For a fundraising round or an acquisition, 24 hours saved have real value. This criterion is often the easiest to assess, and it is the one most often forgotten in the enthusiasm for automation.

The rough rule: if the stakes are low (below 10,000 to 15,000 euros depending on your positioning), automation should be offered at a low price or as a service included in a broader offering. Its purpose is to build the relationship, not to generate margin. If the stakes are significant, a higher fixed fee becomes defensible.

## The degree of standardisation of the service

Some documents are templates: the structure does not change, only the data does. A standard employment contract, general terms and conditions for an online shop, internal regulations: automation makes sense there without any loss of quality.

Other services are by nature personalised. The restructuring of a group, the negotiation of an agreement between partners with diverging interests, advice in judicial reorganisation proceedings: here, automation adds little on the substance. It can prepare the ground, but it cannot replace judgement. If you automate these services, the client perceives it, and is right to perceive it.

## Real urgency

The last criterion is also the trickiest, because it rests on an often debatable assumption: the idea that the client would be willing to pay more to receive their service faster because they are stuck without it.

Real urgency exists. A due diligence to be delivered within 72 hours, a closing signing tomorrow: in these cases, speed has a direct value and the client knows it. But in everyday practice, most clients anticipate their needs a little and are able to wait a reasonable time. The speed premium only works if the client knows in concrete terms what waiting costs them. That is rarer than you might think.

## How to use these four criteria

Give each of these criteria a score from 1 to 3. The total gives an indication.

Between 4 and 6, the potential for automation is low. Traditional handling, or declining if the stakes do not justify the time spent.

Between 7 and 9, the potential is partial. It is possible to automate the first draft and validate it manually, with two levels of service offered to the client.

Between 10 and 12, the potential is high. Automation makes sense, and the fee can be indexed to the stakes rather than to the time spent.

This scoring is not a magic formula. It is a tool for structuring a decision that, without it, is often made on instinct, with the biases that entails.

## My advice

Before deciding to automate a service, take five minutes to score these four criteria. If you do not know what score to give for perceived complexity, it is probably because you have not yet asked your clients how they experience this question. That is often where the real positioning work begins.
