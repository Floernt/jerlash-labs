The four-criteria grid (described in a previous article) gives each service a score. But a score does not tell you how to position the product, or at what price to offer it. For that, you need a second tool.

The horizontal axis is the degree of standardisation of the service. The vertical axis is the level of stakes in the situation. Crossing the two gives four quadrants, each with its own distinct service and pricing logic.

## Quadrant 1: High standardisation, high stakes — the premium product

This is the most interesting quadrant for a firm that wants to start automating. The service is structured enough to be automated without loss of quality, and the stakes are high enough for the client to accept paying a serious fee.

*Example: a shareholders’ agreement for a seed-stage startup with two founders with a standard profile. The structure of the document is known, the variables are limited, and the stakes for the founders are real. Automation produces a good-quality draft, an expert review adds the value of judgement, and the whole thing can be delivered within 48 hours for a fixed fee set according to the stakes of the transaction, not the hours spent.*

## Quadrant 2: Low standardisation, high stakes — the core business

Here, automation only prepares the ground. It can generate a first draft, gather information, identify standard clauses, but it cannot replace strategic analysis. This is where the lawyer’s time has the most value, and this is where the full hourly rate is best justified.

The temptation is to want to automate these matters too, in order to go faster. That is a mistake. The time saved will be marginal, and the client paying top price for a restructuring or a complex acquisition expects a level of attention that cannot be standardised.

## Quadrant 3: High standardisation, low stakes — the door-opener product

These are standard terms and conditions of sale, template service contracts, termination letters. The service can be one hundred per cent automated, but the stakes do not justify a high fee. The right use of this quadrant is to offer these products at a low price or as a service available within an entry-level offering.

The aim is not the margin on this product. It is starting the relationship. An entrepreneur who orders your customised terms and conditions for 150 euros and is satisfied will come back to you when their stakes are higher. It is a door-opener in the literal sense of the term.

## Quadrant 4: Low standardisation, low stakes — the zone to avoid

This is the trap quadrant. The service is expensive to produce because it is unique, and the client cannot pay the real price because the stakes are low. Neither efficiently automatable, nor profitable in the traditional way.

The honest answer in this quadrant is often to decline, or to offer a time-limited online consultation at a fixed fee. Some firms wear themselves out on these matters because they do not know how to say no, or because they have not formalised their positioning. The matrix helps to get out of this situation from the top rather than putting up with it.

## What the matrix changes in practice

Many entrepreneurial lawyers reason along the lines of “I can do this faster with GenAI, so I will lower my price”. The matrix reverses the reasoning. It starts from the stakes and the type of service to determine the right pricing logic, not the other way round.

Two pricing models naturally emerge from this exercise. The first is stakes-based pricing: the price is set on the value of the situation for the client, not on the time you spent. An agreement delivered in 45 minutes for a 500,000-euro transaction can be billed at 1,500 euros, because the client is not buying your time, they are buying legal certainty over 500,000 euros. The second is service-level pricing: for the same service, you offer an automated version at a reduced price and a version with a personalised review at full price. The client chooses with full knowledge of the facts, and cannot blame you for having pressed a button.

## Key takeaways

The devaluation of expertise does not happen when you automate. It happens when you present an automated product as the same thing as your usual work, just done faster. The matrix helps avoid this confusion by structuring the offering so that each level of service has its own logic and its own price.
